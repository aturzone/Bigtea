The enclosure of common land in eighteenth-century England is often narrated as a straightforward story of agricultural improvement, but the archival record complicates that account considerably. Parliamentary enclosure acts did raise yields in many parishes, and the consolidation of scattered strips into compact holdings genuinely reduced the labour lost to travel between them. Yet the same acts extinguished customary rights of pasture, gleaning, and fuel gathering that had underwritten the subsistence of cottagers who owned no land at all and therefore received no allotment in compensation. Historians disagree sharply about the net effect on rural wages, and the disagreement turns less on ideology than on which parishes survive in the record and how one weights them. Explain the main positions and what evidence would settle the question.
